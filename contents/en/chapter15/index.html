Chapter overview. This chapter introduces microlocal analysis as the framework in which regularity is no longer treated as a merely global property of a function, but as a phenomenon that must be resolved simultaneously in physical space and frequency space. The guiding question is therefore more refined than asking whether a solution $$u$$ is smooth: we ask where the singularities of $$u$$ are located inside the cotangent bundle $$T^*X \setminus 0$$, and by what geometric law those singularities propagate under the action of a differential or pseudodifferential operator.

Methodologically, Chapter 15 develops the point of view in which Fourier analysis, symplectic geometry, and linear PDE theory become part of a single language. The wave front set $$\operatorname{WF}(u)$$ records not only the position of singular behavior but also its oscillatory directions. From this perspective, theorems on propagation of singularities show that for hyperbolic operators singularities travel along Hamiltonian bicharacteristics associated with the principal symbol, while microlocal elliptic regularity explains why smoothness is recovered wherever that principal symbol remains nonvanishing. These statements are not isolated technical results; they are the geometric grammar of modern PDE.

The chapter also makes clear why microlocal analysis matters beyond abstract theory. Fourier integral operators provide the natural representation of wave propagation, reflection, scattering, and high-frequency asymptotics. Semiclassical analysis, with parameter $$h \to 0$$, links quantum dynamics to classical trajectories and clarifies how spectral and dynamical information are encoded in oscillatory states. Inverse problems and spectral asymptotics then reveal the applied power of the subject: the same microlocal tools govern what can be reconstructed in tomography, what artifacts appear in imaging, how singularities move through seismic media, and how eigenvalue distributions reflect the underlying geometry of a system.

The internal order of the lessons mirrors the conceptual maturation of the field. We begin with wave front sets and propagation of singularities to establish the geometric meaning of singular support in phase space. We then move to Fourier integral operators and semiclassical analysis, where oscillatory phenomena are organized by canonical relations and high-frequency limits. From there the course reaches spectral asymptotics, inverse problems, and quantum mechanics, which show how microlocal thinking operates in active research areas. The chapter closes with microlocal elliptic theory and current research directions, allowing students to transition from advanced coursework into the style and vocabulary of the modern literature.

Students will get the most from this chapter if they are already comfortable with distributions, the Fourier transform, weak solutions of PDEs, the symbol calculus for pseudodifferential operators, and basic spectral ideas. The intended outcome is not only the ability to parse statements such as “microlocal elliptic regularity” or “propagation of singularities,” but also the deeper insight needed to see why such theorems are the natural answers to geometric questions about concentration, transport of oscillation, and the qualitative structure of solutions.

In the architecture of the course as a whole, Chapter 15 serves as the bridge from classical solution methods to the research-level language of phase space analysis. Earlier chapters teach how to solve equations and estimate solutions; this chapter teaches how to read the geometry of singularities encoded in those solutions. That shift in viewpoint is what makes microlocal analysis one of the central languages of contemporary analysis, mathematical physics, and inverse theory.
