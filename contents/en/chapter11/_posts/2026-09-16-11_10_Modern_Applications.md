## Objectives

This optional lesson places Laplace and Poisson equations, mean-value properties, and Green’s functions next to neural operators and PINNs for elliptic problems. Students should be able to treat Darcy flow and electrostatics as operator-learning benchmarks, use the maximum principle as a hard test, and locate the 2021–2024 papers that made elliptic maps the standard proving ground for FNO, DeepONet, and PINO. The classical potential theory of the chapter is left in place.

## Prerequisites

Students should know Laplace’s equation, Poisson sources, the mean-value property, the maximum principle, and the idea of a Green’s function on a disk or a rectangle.

## Introduction

An elliptic equation does not evolve. It balances. The solution at a point is an average of neighboring values, or a potential generated by distant sources, and it attains its extrema on the boundary unless a source forces otherwise. That global dependence is exactly why elliptic maps became the favorite test problems of operator learning: the map from a coefficient or a source to a potential is nonlocal, smoothing, and sensitive to geometry.

The Darcy flow problem—$$-\nabla\cdot(a\nabla u)=f$$ with a highly oscillatory permeability $$a$$—is the running example of Li et al.’s Fourier neural operator (ICLR 2021) and of Kovachki et al. (*JMLR* 2023). Physics-informed DeepONets (Wang, Wang, and Perdikaris, *Science Advances* 2021) and PINO (Li et al., [arXiv:2111.03794](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.03794)) add residual information so that a coarse data set can still produce a high-resolution potential. PINNacle (Hao et al., NeurIPS 2024) includes elliptic and near-elliptic tasks among its public comparisons.

Electrostatics and incompressible irrotational flow, already in this chapter, are the same operator in different notation. A student who can write the Green’s function on a disk already knows the object these networks are trying to learn.

## Key Concepts

### Coefficient-to-solution operators

For Poisson’s equation with fixed conductivity the operator is linear and is given by a Green’s kernel. For Darcy flow the operator $$a\mapsto u$$ is nonlinear. Neural operators are built for the second case: they must generalize across a family of coefficients, not merely invert one Laplacian. The mean-value property is then no longer exact, but a comparison principle still is, and it remains a valid test.

### Smoothing and super-resolution

Elliptic regularity says that $$u$$ is smoother than $$a$$ or $$f$$ in Sobolev scales, up to the boundary. A model that cannot refine a coarse potential, or that invents new oscillations under mesh refinement, is not behaving like an elliptic inverse. PINO’s selling point is precisely that a physics residual at high resolution can restore this smoothing even when the training snapshots are coarse.

### Maximum and mean-value tests

If $$f=0$$ and the boundary data lie between $$m$$ and $$M$$, a learned potential that exits $$[m,M]$$ is ineligible. If the domain is a disk and $$a\equiv 1$$, the mean-value property is quantitative: the value at the center is the boundary integral. These tests do not require a reference solver.

### Geometry

Rectangles, disks, and exterior domains use different Green’s functions. Geo-FNO (Li, Huang, Huang, and Anandkumar, *J. Comput. Phys.* 2023) learns a deformation toward a regular reference domain so that an FFT-based operator can still be used. That is a modern echo of the conformal-mapping and polar-coordinate techniques of this chapter.

## Methods and Solution Techniques

An elliptic learning workflow:

1. Fix the family of coefficients or sources and the boundary map.
2. Generate training pairs with a finite-element or spectral solver.
3. Train FNO, DeepONet, or PINO; optionally add a residual loss at a finer grid.
4. Validate with maximum/mean-value tests, flux conservation, and a new geometry or contrast ratio.
5. Inspect a learned Green’s slice $$f\mapsto u(\cdot;f=\delta)$$ when the problem is linear.

PINNs remain useful for a single irregular domain or for inverse source identification, but they are usually inferior to operator models when hundreds of similar Poisson problems must be solved.

## Examples

### Example 1: Recovering the disk Green’s function

On the unit disk with Dirichlet data, the Poisson kernel and the Green’s function are explicit. Training a linear operator model on random sources and then probing it with an approximate delta should reproduce the image-charge kernel. Deviation near the boundary is a statement about how well the model learned the image principle.

### Example 2: High-contrast Darcy

If $$a$$ takes two values that differ by several orders of magnitude, the potential develops steep gradients along interfaces. An FNO trained on mild contrasts will smear those interfaces. The failure is an elliptic regularity statement: the solution lives in a weaker space when $$a$$ is only bounded and elliptic, not smooth.

### Example 3: Discrete mean-value check

```python
import numpy as np

# 5-point Laplacian nullspace test on a small grid
u = np.array([[0., 0., 0.],
              [0., 1., 0.],
              [0., 0., 0.]])
resid = 4 * u[1, 1] - u[1, 0] - u[1, 2] - u[0, 1] - u[2, 1]
print(resid)
```

A learned discrete potential for Laplace’s equation should make this residual small in the interior. The five-point stencil is the mean-value property on a grid.

## Applications in Science, Engineering, and Modern Contexts

Subsurface flow, electrostatic device design, incompressible potential flow, and medical electrical-impedance tomography are elliptic or close to elliptic. Operator-learning surrogates turn a finite-element digital twin into an interactive map from permeability or charge density to potential. Inverse elliptic problems—recovering $$a$$ or a source from boundary measurements—are the Calderón problem and its relatives; neural inverse operators and score-based posterior sampling are the 2022–2024 tools of choice, always subject to the identifiability limits that potential theory already knows.

The fluid-flow optional lesson in this chapter (irrotational, incompressible flow) is a direct application: a learned stream function or potential must remain harmonic, so its Laplacian residual is a physics loss with a classical name.

## Challenges and Extensions

High contrast, anisotropy, and vanishing ellipticity break uniform estimates and defeat models trained on milder families. Corners and mixed boundary conditions produce singularities that a global Fourier basis will Gibbs-oscillate. Inverse conductivity remains severely ill-posed; a sharp image from a network is not a uniqueness proof. Three-dimensional problems strain GPU memory for FFT-based operators. Coupling to transport or to Stokes flow leaves the purely elliptic chapter.

If a learned potential satisfies the maximum principle but not flux continuity across an interface, which variational identity has failed? The chapter’s Green’s identities give the answer.

## Exercises

1. **Mean-value test.** For a harmonic polynomial on the disk, write the mean-value identity and turn it into a numerical acceptance test for a learned model.

2. **Green’s operator linearity.** If $$\mathcal{G}(f)$$ is the Dirichlet Poisson solver, what identities must a learned $$\mathcal{G}_\theta$$ satisfy? Design two experiments, one for linearity and one for positivity.

3. **Contrast and regularity.** Explain, using only elliptic intuition, why doubling the contrast of $$a$$ can more than double the gradient of $$u$$ near an interface.

4. **Computational experiment.** Solve $$-u''=f$$ on $$(0,1)$$ with $$u(0)=u(1)=0$$ for many random $$f$$, train a linear map on nodal values, and compare the learned matrix with the discrete Green’s matrix.

5. **Open exploration.** Read the Darcy experiments in Li et al. (ICLR 2021) or Kovachki et al. (2023) and rewrite one figure caption in the language of this chapter (maximum principle, Green’s function, regularity).
