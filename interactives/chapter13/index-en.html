Chapter 13 Interactives

This page brings together the main interactive illustrations for Chapter 13: Euler, Runge-Kutta, multistep methods, stiff equations, finite differences for PDEs, the CFL condition, 1D finite elements, and Euler-Maruyama for SDEs. Each demo is chosen to make the numerical intuition visible before the formal theory.

13-01 Euler's Method

This is the gateway to numerical thinking: a differential equation is replaced by a discrete update rule. The illustration focuses on the relation between step size, global error, and tangent-line intuition.

Forward Euler: Exact Versus Numerical Solution

Teaching objective. See directly how the step size affects approximation quality.
Mathematical idea. Euler uses the tangent line at the beginning of each step to approximate a curved trajectory.
Interactions. Adjust lambda, step size, and the initial value.

13-02 Runge-Kutta Methods

Runge-Kutta methods show that accuracy can improve dramatically by sampling more slope information inside a single time step.

RK4 Versus Euler

Teaching objective. Make the practical benefit of multiple stages visible.
Mathematical idea. RK4 combines intermediate slopes to match local dynamics more faithfully.
Interactions. Adjust the coefficient a, the step size, and the initial value.
Open standalone

13-03 Multistep Methods

Multistep methods emphasize the idea of reusing recent history rather than always taking new slope samples inside each step. That is a different philosophy from Runge-Kutta.

Adams-Bashforth 2

Teaching objective. Make numerical memory visible in the update formula.
Mathematical idea. The new step uses the current slope and the previous slope to approximate the vector field integral.
Interactions. Adjust the coefficient a, the step size, and the initial value.
Open standalone

13-04 Stiff Equations

Stiff equations show students that accuracy is not the only criterion. The stability region of a method can be the dominant issue in choosing a time step.

Forward and Backward Euler on a Stiff Test Problem

Teaching objective. Compare explicit and implicit behavior directly in a stiff setting.
Mathematical idea. The amplification factor of forward Euler may leave the stability region even though the exact solution decays smoothly.
Interactions. Adjust the negative eigenvalue and the step size.
Open standalone

13-05 Finite Differences for PDEs

Here a continuous PDE is replaced by a discrete grid model. The space-time heatmap lets students see how a thermal profile spreads and smooths across the mesh.

Explicit Heat Scheme on a 1D Grid

Teaching objective. Connect a finite-difference stencil to the full space-time numerical evolution.
Mathematical idea. Each new node value depends only on nearby nodes, but stability still depends strongly on the parameter r.
Interactions. Adjust r and the number of time steps.
Open standalone

13-06 Stability, Convergence, and the CFL Condition

The CFL condition is one of the clearest intuitions in numerical PDEs. The illustration below lets students vary the CFL number and watch the transition from stable transport to visible breakdown.

The CFL Condition in the Upwind Scheme

Teaching objective. Tie the definition of CFL to an actual numerical profile.
Mathematical idea. Once the CFL threshold is exceeded, the numerical grid no longer keeps pace with physical information propagation.
Interactions. Adjust the CFL number directly.
Open standalone

13-07 Introduction to Finite Elements

Finite elements differ from finite differences because they begin with an approximation space and local basis functions. This illustration focuses on the intuition of hat functions in one dimension.

Hat Basis Functions in 1D FEM

Teaching objective. See why local basis functions lead naturally to sparse matrices.
Mathematical idea. Each basis function is nonzero only on a very small neighborhood around its node.
Interactions. Adjust the number of mesh nodes and the highlighted basis function.
Open standalone

13-08 Stochastic Differential Equations

This illustration emphasizes the key qualitative difference between ODEs and SDEs: the same initial condition produces many sample paths rather than one deterministic trajectory.

Euler-Maruyama for Geometric Brownian Motion

Teaching objective. Distinguish a single sample path from average behavior.
Mathematical idea. Brownian noise turns the solution into a family of random trajectories rather than one fixed curve.
Interactions. Adjust mu, sigma, the number of paths, and regenerate the noise.
